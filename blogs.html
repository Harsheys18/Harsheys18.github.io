Working Is Not the Same as Being Right

Abstract In most labs, "it works" means a number went up. But scoring well on average and behaving correctly every time are two different claims, and we have quietly started treating them as one. I look at where that gap actually bites — rare, expensive failures in robotics — and at what I found during a summer on safe RL: nearly every method turns a hard safety constraint into a soft one somewhere in the maths. Verification is hard and does not scale yet. That is a research problem, not a reason to change what we are aiming at.

What Benchmarks Quietly Decide

Abstract Somebody picks the number a field competes on, and that choice quietly decides which problems get worked on and which failures are even visible. I walk through three cases I have watched up close: safety benchmarks that can only add costs up, image metrics that confuse "realistic" with "good", and a robotics field that knows about the sim-to-real gap and keeps testing in simulation anyway. Plus four habits that make benchmarks less misleading without throwing them out.

Built for Whom?

Abstract Software assumes things about you before you ever open it: your bandwidth, your language, your device, whether your power stays on. Those assumptions are invisible to anyone they happen to be true for. Starting from a first-year startup idea that went nowhere, I argue that building for the harder case is not charity — it makes better software for everyone — and that large models make this worse than earlier technology did, because you can no longer work around a bad assumption locally.

In Praise of Doing It Badly First

Abstract I have read papers I was sure I understood, then failed to write the first twenty lines of code. Reading gives you a convincing feeling of understanding that is not the same as understanding. Using an HMM tagger, a diffusion model, and a Flappy Bird agent I built for students I was mentoring, I make the case for deliberately bad first versions — and for why "one more paper before I start" is almost always avoidance wearing a responsible face.
